New Risks, Not Fewer: Data, Technology and Fairness in Human Rights Investigations

Hancock et al. (2024) show that digital technologies widen the reach of human rights investigations without narrowing the distance between investigator and context. Satellite imagery, AIS vessel tracking and remote sensing produce evidence beyond fieldwork's reach, yet interpretation remains human judgement performed far from the communities concerned. The technology does not reduce risk; it changes the risk profile, creating fairness and accountability failures that must be treated at the design stage.

Fairness falters on objectivity: datasets are gathered and interpreted by actors whose assumptions and blind spots shape what is knowable (boyd and Crawford, 2012), so unrecorded harms are systematically overlooked. Accountability falters on opacity: where conclusions rest on proprietary tools and algorithmic assessments, users cannot verify how a judgement was reached (Burrell, 2016).

Building such systems without their communities poses three risks: extraction, where communities supply evidence but are excluded from decisions on its use (Couldry and Mejias, 2019); misaligned threat models, where designers guard against visible harms while communities absorb invisible ones, such as retaliation and surveillance; and eroded trust in findings communities did not help create. The mitigations are design choices: participatory collaboration rather than consultation (D'Ignazio and Klein, 2020), independent toolchain audit (Raji et al., 2020), and data minimisation with informed consent.

For risk managers, a control not built with the people it protects is itself a new risk.
