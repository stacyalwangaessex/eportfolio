Peer Response

Asma, I agree with your analysis, particularly the two concepts you draw from Hancock et al. (2024). The "false sense of bright spots" is the risk I find most concerning: an investigator relying on satellite coverage can mistake the absence of detected violations for the absence of violations, quietly reallocating attention towards data-rich regions and away from the places where abuse may be most likely. Your "accountability horizon" point raises the same difficulty in another form — when data passes between several organisations, responsibility for misuse becomes diffuse and, in practice, unowned.

Building on your mitigations, I would locate the preventive work in the evidential standard itself. Koenig et al. (2021) argue that digital evidence should support rather than replace traditional investigative methods, and writing that principle into protocols from the outset prevents survivor testimony and local knowledge from being marginalised by default. Your call for human oversight is strongest when it is documented: a named reviewer recording what the data could not show turns bright spots into acknowledged limits of coverage rather than findings of safety.

A question back to you: should the absence of data be treated as inadmissible unless its coverage limitations are documented?
