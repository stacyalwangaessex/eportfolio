**Summary Post**

Three weeks of discussion have strengthened my position that CVSS's weaknesses are structural rather than behavioural. My initial post accepted Wunder et al.'s (2024) three criticisms — inconsistent evaluation of the Attack Vector, User Interaction and Scope metrics; the mis-scoring of non-exploitable security deficiencies; and neglected, dispersed documentation — and argued SSVC should replace CVSS because it answers the remediation-urgency question directly while sidestepping the contested metrics.

The peer discussion broadened this in two ways. Janet's severity-versus-risk distinction reframed the problem: CVSS measures technical severity, whereas prioritisation decisions depend on exploitation likelihood and business context, which is why she argued for EPSS. Her observation that inconsistent scores undermine the allocation of limited remediation resources connected the academic critique to operational consequence. Abinel went further and proposed a hybrid: exploitation probability as the primary prioritisation mechanism with CVSS severity retained as complementary information, so that likelihood and consequence are both represented. His reading of the study's attitude data — 85% of users consider CVSS inconsistent yet 80% still find it useful — captured CVSS's real role: a flawed but valued common language.

My thinking changed on replacement. I initially argued for SSVC replacing CVSS outright; the discussion moved me towards complementarity. Jacobs et al. (2021) show exploitation probability predicts real-world prioritisation better than severity alone, but no single instrument covers likelihood, consequence and stakeholder context simultaneously. The defensible synthesis is a layered approach: empirical exploitation data drives ordering, a documented decision rule governs how probability and impact are weighed together, and the choice of that rule — and its ownership — is made explicit rather than left to intuition. The transferable lesson for risk practice is that no scoring instrument, however empirical, substitutes for a documented, auditable decision process — and that instrument design deserves the same scrutiny as the judgements it is meant to structure.
