**Summary Post**

Three weeks of discussion have confirmed the central claim of my initial post: data and technology do not reduce risk in human rights investigations, they change its profile. The risks cluster around two failures. Fairness fails through partial representation: satellite imagery, AIS data and open-source intelligence cover some populations and spaces better than others, so violations in data-poor regions become systematically less visible. Accountability fails through opacity and diffusion: when conclusions rest on proprietary tools and data passes between organisations, responsibility for errors and misuse becomes difficult to assign.

The peer discussion sharpened this in two ways. Kaona's account of the accountability gap — investigations that appear rigorous because they are data-driven while concealing uncertain assumptions — showed how partial data can manufacture unwarranted confidence, and her distinction between consultation and shared decision-making power reframed participation as an authority question, not a process question. Asma's reading of Hancock et al. (2024) added two mechanisms I had underweighted: the "false sense of bright spots", where absence of detected violations is mistaken for absence of violations, and the "accountability horizon" that diffuses responsibility across organisations.

My thinking changed on where mitigation belongs. I initially located preventive work in technical controls — data minimisation, retention rules, independent audit. The discussion moved me towards the evidential and governance layer: Koenig et al. (2021) argue digital evidence should support rather than replace traditional methods, and Katell et al. (2020) show participatory design surfaces harms that occur even when systems work as intended. The synthesis is that governance must precede collection, not follow deployment: communities need genuine decision-making authority, coverage limitations need documenting before any finding of safety, and the absence of data needs treating as a limit of knowledge rather than evidence of safety. These conclusions transfer directly to risk management practice, where controls built without the people they protect become new risks.
